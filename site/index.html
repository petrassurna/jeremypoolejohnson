Most people who contact me start with an apology.

They haven’t played since school. They think they’ve left it too late. They’re worried about wasting a professional’s time.

None of that is a problem. It’s just what starting looks like.

  • No exams, unless you actually want one.
  • No scales for the sake of scales.
  • No recitals.
  • No requirement to read music.
  • No being told off for not practising enough.

How it actually works

One

We find out what you want to play

A song, a style, an old piece you never finished. That’s the target, and everything we do points at it.

Two

We start with chords

Not five years of notation first. Chords mean you’re making something that sounds like music in the first few weeks.

Three

Technique arrives as you need it

Reading, theory, fingering — all of it comes in when it solves a problem you actually have. That way it sticks.

The questions people are too polite to ask

Am I too old?

No. Adults learn differently from children, not worse. You’ll understand in a week of harmony what takes a seven-year-old a year, because you already understand how things fit together. What children have is time and no self-consciousness — and we can work around the second one.

Do I need to read music?

No. Plenty of my students play from chord symbols and never read a note of notation. If you want to learn to read, we’ll do that too — but it’s a tool, not an entry requirement.

How much do I need to practise?

Twenty minutes, most days, is plenty. Genuinely. Short and frequent beats an anxious two-hour session on a Sunday every time, and I’d much rather you kept going than burned out in week six.

I played as a kid and forgot everything. Am I back to square one?

You’re not. This is the most reassuring true thing I can tell you: it comes back far faster than it went in. The muscle memory is still there under the dust, and you’ll be past your childhood level sooner than you expect.

I don’t have a piano.

Not a blocker. A second-hand weighted 88-key digital piano will get you a long way, and I’m happy to tell you exactly what to look for before you spend anything. Don’t buy anything before your first lesson — ask me at it.

What actually happens in the first lesson?

We talk for a few minutes about what you’d like to play. You sit at the piano. We do something small that works. You leave with one thing to try. There’s no test, and nobody has ever embarrassed themselves.
